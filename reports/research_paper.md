# Manufacturing Process Health and Operational Efficiency Analysis in 6G-Enabled Smart Factories

### A diagnostic baseline for 100,000 machine-minutes of industrial IIoT telemetry, and a validation protocol for the data that precedes predictive AI

**Domain:** Industrial IIoT · Smart Manufacturing · 6G-connected production systems
**Dataset:** `Thales_Group_Manufacturing.csv` — 100,000 readings, 50 machines, 1 Jan – 10 Mar 2025
**Deliverables:** research paper · live Streamlit analytics dashboard · executive summary
**Reproducibility:** every figure and statistic in this paper is regenerated by `python analysis/run_eda.py`

---

## Abstract

Modern smart factories instrument hundreds of machines with real-time IIoT sensors and, increasingly, carry that telemetry over low-latency 6G links. Before such a plant can justify investment in predictive maintenance or AI-driven optimisation, three questions must be answered from the data itself: *are machines operating within healthy ranges, where are efficiency losses occurring, and which machines show early signs of degradation?*

This study builds the diagnostic intelligence layer that answers those questions and applies it to a 100,000-reading corpus covering 50 machines across three operation modes. We define and implement five operational KPIs — a composite Machine Health Index, Average Production Speed, Defect Density Score, Error Frequency Index, and the Efficiency Distribution — and deliver them through a live dashboard with machine, mode, time and metric filters.

Applying that layer produces a clear and, we argue, more valuable result than a predictive model would have. Three findings dominate. **First, the nine sensor channels are mutually and jointly independent:** the largest absolute Pearson correlation among all 36 sensor pairs is 0.0075, and every channel is statistically indistinguishable from a uniform distribution across its full valid range (Kolmogorov–Smirnov *p* > 0.05 for eight of nine). **Second, the `Efficiency_Status` target is not a measurement but a deterministic arithmetic rule** applied to two columns already present in the file — production speed and error rate — which we recover exactly and which reproduces the published label for 99.998% of rows. **Third, and consequently, the physical sensors carry no information about efficiency at all:** a random forest given the seven physical and network channels achieves a balanced accuracy of 0.3333 on three classes, precisely the value of random guessing, and performs identically when the labels are randomly shuffled (difference: −0.00003).

We therefore report that this corpus cannot support supervised efficiency prediction, condition-based maintenance, or machine benchmarking, and we show *why* using evidence that any reviewer can re-run. We quantify the trap this creates: the same model reports 77.8% raw accuracy, a figure that would survive most project reviews while encoding nothing but the class imbalance. We also document two structural defects in the file — a duplicated capture day producing 2,880 repeated timestamps, and a factory-wide sampling cadence that observes each machine only once every ~50 minutes on average. The paper closes with the instrumentation, sampling and validation requirements a real deployment must satisfy, and with a six-check acceptance protocol that would have caught every issue reported here before a single model was trained.

**Keywords:** industrial IIoT, smart manufacturing, 6G networks, machine health monitoring, data validation, target leakage, feature ablation, synthetic data detection

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and context

A modern smart factory operates with hundreds of interconnected machines, real-time Industrial IoT sensor streams, and a high dependency on stable production quality. The arrival of 6G-class connectivity changes what is feasible: sub-10-millisecond latencies and dense device support make it practical to stream per-machine telemetry continuously rather than polling a supervisory system every few minutes. That capability creates an obvious temptation — to move directly from "we now have the data" to "let us train a model on it."

Manufacturing leaders are told, correctly, that AI can predict failures and optimise throughput. What is less often said is that those techniques rest on a precondition: the telemetry must actually encode the physical process. A vibration channel must rise when a bearing wears. A thermal channel must respond to load. If those couplings are absent — because of sensor placement, because of a broken ETL stage, because a field was synthesised to fill a gap — then every downstream model learns the shape of the noise, and does so while reporting metrics that look entirely respectable.

### 1.2 Problem statement

Despite holding large volumes of real-time sensor and production data, manufacturing teams routinely struggle with four specific failures:

1. **Lack of centralised visibility** into machine health across the fleet.
2. **Difficulty correlating sensor behaviour** with production efficiency.
3. **Reactive handling** of defects and errors, discovered after they affect output.
4. **Inability to benchmark machines** against operational standards.

The result is that inefficiencies go unnoticed until they impact production output or quality.

### 1.3 Objectives and scope

This project builds the diagnostic layer that must precede any predictive initiative. Concretely, it aims to:

- validate the telemetry and quantify its fitness for analytics;
- establish machine-level sensor health baselines and identify machines near threshold limits;
- diagnose production performance and locate underperforming assets;
- correlate quality and error behaviour with sensor conditions;
- characterise the efficiency distribution across machines, modes and shifts;
- deliver all of the above through a live, filterable dashboard.

It explicitly does **not** aim to deliver a predictive maintenance model. Section 5 explains why that would have been the wrong deliverable for this corpus, and Section 7 sets out what must change before it becomes the right one.

### 1.4 Contributions

1. A reproducible diagnostic and KPI framework for 6G-enabled manufacturing telemetry, implemented as a live dashboard with four analytical modules.
2. Exact recovery of the `Efficiency_Status` generating rule, demonstrating target circularity.
3. A feature-ablation and permutation protocol that separates genuine predictive signal from class-imbalance artefacts — and a demonstration of how large the gap between the two can be (77.8% vs. chance).
4. Identification of two structural data defects (duplicate capture window; inadequate per-machine sampling cadence) with quantified operational impact.
5. A six-check data acceptance protocol for industrial telemetry, intended for use before AI investment is committed.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Source and shape

The corpus is a single CSV of **100,000 readings** across **14 columns**, covering **50 machines** (IDs 1–50) between **1 January 2025 00:00** and **10 March 2025 10:39**.

| Column | Description | Observed range |
|---|---|---|
| `Date`, `Timestamp` | Calendar date and time of capture | 1 Jan – 10 Mar 2025, 1-minute steps |
| `Machine_ID` | Unique industrial machine identifier | 1 – 50 |
| `Operation_Mode` | Current operating mode | Active (70.1%), Idle (20.1%), Maintenance (9.9%) |
| `Temperature_C` | Operating temperature | 30.00 – 90.00 °C |
| `Vibration_Hz` | Machine vibration frequency | 0.10 – 5.00 Hz |
| `Power_Consumption_kW` | Electrical power consumed | 1.50 – 10.00 kW |
| `Network_Latency_ms` | 6G network communication latency | 1.00 – 50.00 ms |
| `Packet_Loss_%` | Packet loss during machine communication | 0.00 – 5.00 % |
| `Quality_Control_Defect_Rate_%` | Defective units found at QC | 0.00 – 10.00 % |
| `Production_Speed_units_per_hr` | Units produced per hour | 50.00 – 500.00 |
| `Predictive_Maintenance_Score` | AI-derived maintenance readiness | 0.00 – 1.00 |
| `Error_Rate_%` | Operational errors during production | 0.00 – 15.00 % |
| `Efficiency_Status` | Target label | High (3.0%), Medium (19.2%), Low (77.8%) |

There are **no missing values**, **no readings outside their declared envelopes**, and **no fully duplicated records** — a clean bill of health on the checks most pipelines stop at. The defects in this file are structural, and only appear under checks that go further.

### 2.2 Validation and preparation

**Timeline discontinuity.** Parsing `Date` and `Timestamp` into a single datetime and scanning for backwards steps reveals that the file is **not one continuous recording**. It is two concatenated capture blocks:

| Block | Starts | Ends | Rows |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2025-01-01 00:00 | 2025-03-01 23:59 | 86,400 |
| B | 2025-03-01 00:00 | 2025-03-10 10:39 | 13,600 |

Block A is exactly 60 days at one reading per minute (60 × 1440 = 86,400). Block B resumes at the *start* of 1 March, a day already fully covered by block A. **The two blocks overlap by exactly 1,440 minutes**, producing **2,880 rows carrying repeated timestamps** and **56 rows in which a single machine reports two different readings for the same minute**. Those 56 collisions are physically impossible and were resolved by retaining the first record, removing 28 rows and leaving **99,972 validated readings**.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. Any pipeline that concatenates these blobs without a timeline check silently **double-counts a full day of production** — inflating output totals, deflating per-unit costs, and corrupting any daily trend that spans 1 March.

**Sampling cadence.** One reading is written per minute *for the entire factory*, not per machine. Each machine therefore appears in roughly 2,000 of the 100,000 slots, and the interval between consecutive observations of the same machine has a **median of 34 minutes, a mean of 49 minutes, and a maximum of 514 minutes** — over eight hours. Section 7.1 returns to why this alone disqualifies the corpus from condition monitoring.

**Categorical standardisation and imputation.** Operation modes and efficiency statuses were trimmed and case-normalised against a canonical vocabulary; machine identifiers were coerced to integers. Readings falling outside a sensor's physically valid envelope would have been treated as sensor faults and imputed with that machine's own median — in this file, no such reading exists.

### 2.3 Derived features

Six engineered columns support the KPI layer:

- **Stress scores** (thermal, vibration, power): each sensor mapped to 0 while the reading stays better than its warning threshold, ramping linearly to 1 at the edge of the valid envelope.
- **`Machine_Health_Index`** = 100 × (1 − mean stress) over the three mechanical channels.
- **`Defect_Density`** = defect rate × production speed ÷ 100, i.e. *defective units per hour* rather than a bare percentage.
- **`Good_Units_per_hr`** = production speed − defect density.
- **`Breach_Count`**: how many of the nine sensors sit in their worst quartile for a given reading.
- **`Shift`** (Night / Day / Evening) and hour-of-day, for temporal roll-ups.

Warning thresholds were set at the 75th percentile of each observed distribution and are declared in one place (`src/config.py`) so the paper, the analysis pipeline and the dashboard cannot drift apart. They are configurable: a plant engineer would replace them with manufacturer limits.

## 3. Methodology

The analysis follows the six-stage methodology set out in the project brief, with a seventh validation stage added once the first results made it necessary.

1. **Data validation and preparation** — envelope checks, timeline reconstruction, duplicate resolution, categorical standardisation.
2. **Machine-level sensor health analysis** — per-machine temperature, vibration and power profiles; threshold-proximity exposure; stability comparison across operation modes.
3. **Production performance diagnostics** — throughput trends, output consistency (coefficient of variation), identification of underperforming machines against 3σ control limits.
4. **Quality and error analysis** — defect rate against sensor behaviour, error spikes by machine and time, quality bottleneck identification.
5. **Efficiency status distribution** — High/Medium/Low spread across machines, modes and shifts.
6. **Cross-metric diagnostics** — temperature vs. defect rate, vibration vs. error rate, power vs. throughput, network quality vs. errors.
7. **Signal validation** — feature ablation, permutation testing, mutual information, and label-rule recovery.

Two methodological choices deserve comment.

**Binned means over scatter plots.** A scatter plot of 100,000 noisy points is visually uninformative — it fills its bounding box regardless of whether a relationship exists. Every bivariate result here is instead reported as a binned conditional mean with a 95% confidence interval, which makes a flat relationship visibly flat and a real one visibly sloped.

**Balanced accuracy over raw accuracy.** With 77.8% of readings in one class, raw accuracy is dominated by the majority. Every classification result is reported against both the majority-class baseline and the chance level for balanced accuracy (0.3333 for three classes). Section 5.2 shows why this distinction is the single most consequential methodological decision in the study.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Factory health baseline

Across all 99,972 validated readings:

| KPI | Value |
|---|---|
| Machine Health Index (fleet mean) | **87.50 / 100** |
| Average Production Speed | **275.9 units/hr** |
| Defect Density Score | **13.80 defective units/hr** |
| Error Frequency Index | **7.50 %** |
| Efficiency Distribution | **High 2.99% · Medium 19.19% · Low 77.83%** |
| Good units produced | 262.1 units/hr |
| Readings with ≥1 sensor near its limit | 92.6 % |
| Readings with degraded 6G link | 43.9 % |

Two of these headline numbers look alarming on their own and are worth resolving immediately. That 92.6% of readings have at least one of nine sensors in its worst quartile is not a crisis — it is what independence predicts. If nine channels are independent and each is in its worst quartile a quarter of the time, the probability that at least one is flagged is 1 − 0.75⁹ = 92.5%, which matches the observed 92.6% almost exactly. The same arithmetic explains the 43.9% "degraded network" figure (1 − 0.75² = 43.75%). **These are not fleet health signals; they are a direct measurement of sensor independence,** and they are the first quantitative hint of what Section 4.4 confirms.

### 4.2 Machine-level sensor health

Every one of the nine sensors spends between **24.94% and 25.23%** of its readings in its worst quartile — a spread of 0.29 percentage points across nine independent channels. There is no sensor the factory is repeatedly pushing against its limit, and no sensor that stays comfortably clear.

Across the 50 machines, the Machine Health Index ranges from **86.88 to 88.15** — a total spread of **1.27 points on a 100-point scale**. Applying 3σ Shewhart control limits to the per-machine KPI distributions returns **zero machines outside the limits on the Health Index, average production speed, error frequency, or defect density**.

This is a substantive finding, not an absence of one. It means the correct answer to *"which machines show early signs of degradation?"* is **none — and the ranking that a dashboard would display is reshuffled by sampling noise, not by real differences.** A monitoring product that presented machine 8 as "worst" (Health Index 86.88) and machine 35 as "best" (88.15) would be inviting maintenance teams to act on a 1.3-point gap that carries no physical meaning.

### 4.3 Production, quality and error diagnostics

Output consistency is extremely poor: the coefficient of variation of production speed is **47.2%** fleet-wide, and between **45.6% and 49.1%** for every individual machine. In a controlled process, minute-to-minute throughput clusters around a setpoint; here it is drawn from across the entire 50–500 units/hr range.

The classic speed–quality trade-off is absent. The correlation between throughput and defect *rate* is **r = −0.0049**, explaining 0.002% of variance. Defect *density* (defective units per hour) does rise with throughput, but only arithmetically — it is the same percentage applied to a larger number, not a degradation in quality.

Most decisively, bucketing readings by how many sensors sit near their limits shows **no effect on output whatsoever**: readings with six flagged sensors produce at the same rate as readings with none. In a real plant this curve slopes down. Here it is flat.

### 4.4 The sensors are mutually independent

Across all 36 sensor pairs, the **largest absolute Pearson correlation is 0.0075** (vibration vs. network latency), which explains 0.006% of variance. The three strongest associations in the entire matrix are:

| Pair | *r* | Variance explained |
|---|---|---|
| Vibration ↔ 6G latency | +0.0075 | 0.006 % |
| Packet loss ↔ Production speed | −0.0071 | 0.005 % |
| 6G latency ↔ Packet loss | −0.0069 | 0.005 % |

At *n* = 99,972 the standard error of a correlation under independence is ≈ 0.0032, so a maximum of 0.0075 across 36 pairs is exactly the largest value one expects to see by chance. Physically, this is implausible: packet loss and latency on a shared 6G link are causally connected, and power draw and throughput are connected by the physics of doing work. **Their independence here is evidence about how the file was produced, not about the factory.**

Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests against a uniform distribution over each sensor's observed range fail to reject uniformity for eight of the nine channels (*p* > 0.05; production speed is marginal at *p* = 0.022). Real physical processes produce clustered, skewed distributions; flat distributions across an exact round-numbered range (30–90 °C, 0.1–5.0 Hz, 1.5–10.0 kW) are the signature of a random generator.

Neither machine identity, operation mode, shift, nor day of week is associated with efficiency:

| Factor | χ² | dof | *p* | Cramér's V | Association |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Machine_ID | 90.01 | 98 | 0.705 | 0.021 | none |
| Operation_Mode | 3.55 | 4 | 0.471 | 0.004 | none |
| Shift | 5.19 | 4 | 0.269 | 0.005 | none |
| Day of week | 5.28 | 12 | 0.948 | 0.005 | none |

That `Operation_Mode` is independent of efficiency is on its own disqualifying: **a machine in Maintenance mode is classified exactly like a machine running under load.** No physical account of a factory permits that.

The per-machine spread test makes the same point quantitatively. The share of High-efficiency readings ranges from 2.32% to 3.68% across machines, a standard deviation of **0.345 percentage points**. Pure binomial sampling with *no* real differences between machines would produce **0.381 pp**. The observed spread is **0.91× the noise floor** — if anything slightly *less* variable than chance. The equivalent figure for Low-efficiency readings is 1.06×.

### 4.5 The label is a deterministic rule

Fitting a depth-4 decision tree to `Efficiency_Status` using only production speed and error rate recovers the generating rule exactly:

```
High     if  Production_Speed >= 400  and  Error_Rate <= 2
Medium   if  Production_Speed >= 200  and  Error_Rate <= 5
Low      otherwise
```

This reproduces the published label for **99,970 of 99,972 rows (99.998%)**. The two exceptions both sit exactly on the `Error_Rate = 5.000` boundary and are consistent with a strict-versus-inclusive inequality in the original generator.

`Efficiency_Status` is therefore **not a measurement of anything**. It is arithmetic applied to two columns that are themselves in the dataset. Predicting it from those columns is circular; predicting it from the physical sensors is impossible, because they were never part of its definition.

### 4.6 Feature ablation: the decisive experiment

We trained the same random forest on nested feature sets and compared each against both trivial baselines.

| Feature set | *n* | Accuracy | Balanced accuracy | Macro F1 | Beats baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Majority-class baseline | 0 | 0.7783 | 0.3333 | 0.2918 | *reference* |
| **All 9 numeric columns** | 9 | **1.0000** | **1.0000** | **1.0000** | **yes** |
| 7 physical/network sensors only | 7 | 0.7782 | 0.3333 | 0.2918 | **no** |
| **Throughput + error rate only** | 2 | **0.9999** | **0.9995** | **0.9995** | **yes** |
| Temperature + vibration + power | 3 | 0.7753 | 0.3330 | 0.2936 | **no** |
| 6G network quality only | 2 | 0.7638 | 0.3333 | 0.3031 | **no** |

Read the balanced-accuracy column. **Every feature set that excludes the two label-defining columns scores 0.333 — exactly chance on three classes.** Two columns reach 0.9995; the other seven contribute nothing.

Mutual information, which detects non-linear dependence that correlation would miss, agrees: `Error_Rate_%` scores 0.348 bits and `Production_Speed` 0.144 bits, while **every remaining channel scores below 0.002 bits**. ANOVA gives the same verdict as variance explained: error rate accounts for 38.4% of between-class variance and production speed 11.3%, while the seven sensors each explain **less than 0.005%** at *p* > 0.15.

**The permutation test settles it.** We refit the seven-sensor model against a randomly shuffled target. Real labels: 0.77822. Shuffled labels: 0.77824. **Difference: −0.00003.** A model trained on genuine labels performs indistinguishably from one trained on noise — the clearest possible demonstration that those seven channels contain no learnable signal.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What the diagnostic layer actually found

Returning to the three questions the brief poses:

> **Are machines operating within healthy ranges?**
> Yes — uniformly and uninformatively. Every machine sits within 1.27 Health Index points of every other, and no machine breaches a 3σ control limit on any KPI.

> **Where are efficiency losses occurring?**
> Efficiency is defined as low throughput or high error rate, and both are drawn independently at random. Losses are not concentrated in any machine, mode, shift, or day. There is no location to point at.

> **Which machines show early signs of degradation?**
> None. Degradation implies a trend; every sensor here is stationary noise. The per-machine ranking a dashboard would display is reshuffled by sampling noise, and acting on it would waste maintenance capacity.

These are complete answers. They are also the answers that make the fourth objective — benchmarking machines against operational standards — impossible on this corpus, and it is better to state that than to publish a league table with no meaning behind it.

### 5.2 The 77.8% trap

The most transferable result in this study is the gap between two numbers describing the *same model*.

A random forest trained on the seven physical and network sensors achieves **77.8% accuracy**. That number would pass most project reviews. It appears on slides. It supports a business case. It is also **entirely an artefact of class imbalance**: the model has learned to answer "Low" every time, which is correct 77.8% of the time because 77.8% of readings are Low. Its balanced accuracy is 0.3333 — chance — and it performs identically on shuffled labels.

The distance between "77.8% accurate" and "learned nothing" is invisible unless you deliberately look for it. Three inexpensive checks make it visible, and we recommend all three as standing practice:

1. **Always report against the majority-class baseline.** A model that does not beat it has not earned its deployment.
2. **Always report balanced accuracy or macro F1** on imbalanced targets.
3. **Always run a permutation test.** It takes minutes and cannot be argued with.

### 5.3 Target circularity

Even the 100% accuracy achieved using all nine columns is not a success — it is leakage. The model has rediscovered the arithmetic that generated the label. Deploying it would produce a system that consumes measured throughput and error rate in order to output a classification that could have been computed with two comparisons, and that tells an operator nothing they did not already have.

The general lesson: **before modelling a target, establish how it was generated.** If it is a deterministic function of available features, the modelling task is not prediction but re-derivation. Real efficiency labels should come from an independent source — MES production records, QC sign-off, OEE calculations — not from a formula over columns already in the feature table.

### 5.4 Assessing the data's provenance

Four independent lines of evidence point the same way: uniform marginals across exact round-numbered ranges; zero pairwise correlation among channels that are causally coupled in any real plant; complete independence between operation mode and every sensor; and a target that is exactly reproducible by a two-variable rule. Taken together these indicate the sensor columns were **generated by independent random draws** rather than recorded from physical equipment.

We state this as a finding about the data, not a criticism of the exercise. Synthetic data is legitimate and useful for building and testing pipelines — which is precisely what it has been used for here. The failure mode to avoid is treating synthetic telemetry as though it were evidence about a factory. Every component built in this project is real, tested, and ready; what it needs is instrumented data to run against.

### 5.5 Why this is the right deliverable

It would have been straightforward to train a classifier, report 100% accuracy using all nine columns, and present a polished predictive maintenance dashboard. That deliverable would have been worse than useless: it would have created confidence in a signal that does not exist, and the failure would only have surfaced after deployment against real equipment.

The brief describes this project as *"the critical first step before predictive maintenance, efficiency forecasting, or AI-driven optimization initiatives."* Establishing that the current corpus cannot support those initiatives — and specifying exactly what must change — **is** that first step, performed correctly.

## 6. The dashboard

The analytical layer is delivered as a Streamlit application with five modules. All statistics recompute live against the active filter selection.

**Factory Health Overview** — efficiency distribution, the five KPIs benchmarked against fleet baselines, average sensor metrics with threshold-proximity, and time-spent-near-limits.

**Machine Health Dashboard** — per-machine sensor trends at hourly or daily granularity, a health scorecard ranked against 3σ control limits, a transparent breakdown of how the Machine Health Index is composed, and a downloadable 50-machine scorecard.

**Production & Quality Panel** — throughput against defect rate and defect density, error frequency by machine, mode and hour, machine-to-machine comparison, and bottleneck analysis by sensor-breach count.

**Efficiency Diagnostics** — the recovered label rule shown in throughput/error space, the feature-ablation study, mode and shift comparisons, an interactive any-metric-vs-any-metric explorer with binned means, and the full statistical test suite.

**Data Quality & Validation** — every validation check, the capture-block timeline, the duplicate-window warning, and sampling-cadence diagnostics.

User controls span machine selection, operation mode, date range, hour-of-day, efficiency status, and metric comparison toggles, satisfying the brief's user-capability requirements.

Critically, the dashboard **does not hide the null results**. Where the fleet is statistically homogeneous it says so, and explains what the noise floor is. A monitoring tool that manufactures alarms from noise trains its operators to ignore it.

## 7. Recommendations

### 7.1 Instrumentation and data collection

**Sample per machine, not per factory.** The current file writes one reading per minute for the whole plant, leaving each machine unobserved for a median of 34 minutes and up to 8.5 hours. A bearing can fail inside that gap. Condition monitoring requires a fixed per-machine cadence — 1 Hz or faster for vibration, 0.1 Hz for thermal.

**Capture vibration as a spectrum, not a scalar.** A single "Vibration_Hz" number discards the frequency content that makes vibration diagnostic. Bearing, imbalance and misalignment faults are identified by *which* band the energy sits in, which requires an FFT or at minimum banded RMS values.

**Preserve physical coupling through the pipeline.** Power draw must move with load; latency and packet loss must move together on a shared link. If those relationships are absent downstream, the ETL stage is destroying them — verify coupling at ingestion, not at analysis.

**Enforce timeline integrity at ingestion.** Reject or quarantine any batch whose timestamps overlap an existing window. The 1 March duplication in this file would have been caught by a single monotonicity assertion.

### 7.2 Target definition

**Source efficiency labels independently of the feature table.** Use MES output records, QC sign-off, or a standard OEE decomposition (availability × performance × quality). A label computed from columns already in the feature set cannot be predicted, only re-derived.

**Record label provenance as metadata.** Every target column should carry a documented definition, owner, and generation method. Had that metadata accompanied this file, Section 4.5 would have been unnecessary.

### 7.3 A six-check acceptance protocol

We recommend the following gate before any industrial dataset is admitted to a modelling programme. Each check is inexpensive; together they would have caught every issue in this study.

| # | Check | Pass criterion | This dataset |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Timeline integrity** | Timestamps monotonic per machine; no overlapping capture windows | ✗ 1,440-minute overlap |
| 2 | **Sampling adequacy** | Per-machine interval below the fault-development time constant | ✗ 34 min median |
| 3 | **Distribution realism** | Sensors show clustered, skewed distributions, not uniform ones | ✗ 8/9 uniform |
| 4 | **Physical coupling** | Causally linked channels correlate as physics requires | ✗ max \|r\| = 0.0075 |
| 5 | **Target independence** | Label is not a deterministic function of available features | ✗ 99.998% recovered |
| 6 | **Signal validation** | Model beats majority baseline on balanced accuracy; fails on shuffled labels | ✗ 0.333 = chance |

A dataset failing checks 1–2 needs an engineering fix. Failing 3–4 indicates the telemetry is not measuring the process. Failing 5–6 means the modelling task as posed is not a modelling task.

### 7.4 Re-running this analysis on real data

Every component here is written against column names, not against the specific values in this file. Point `src/config.py` at instrumented telemetry with the same schema and the entire pipeline — validation, KPIs, dashboard, statistical suite, ablation study — runs unchanged. The tests in Section 4 then become genuinely diagnostic: correlations should be non-zero, distributions should be skewed, the ablation should show sensors beating baseline, and machines should separate under control limits. **The analysis is designed so that "the data is fine" is a result it can produce.**

## 8. Limitations

- **Threshold selection.** Warning thresholds are set at observed 75th percentiles rather than manufacturer limits, because no equipment specifications accompany the dataset. They are declared in one configuration file and are intended to be replaced.
- **Health index weighting.** The Machine Health Index weights thermal, vibration and power stress equally. Real weightings should be derived from failure-mode analysis for the specific equipment class.
- **Single corpus.** All conclusions concern this file. They should not be read as claims about any operational facility, any vendor's equipment, or 6G-enabled manufacturing in general.
- **Provenance inference.** Section 5.4 infers synthetic generation from statistical evidence. That inference is strong and multiply supported, but it is an inference; the authoritative answer lies with whoever produced the file.
- **No causal claims.** This is an observational diagnostic study. Even had correlations been present, establishing causal drivers of efficiency would require controlled intervention.

## 9. Conclusion

This project set out to build a diagnostic intelligence layer for 6G-enabled smart manufacturing — a centralised view of machine health, production efficiency and quality indicators that would let an operator move from reactive issue handling to data-driven operational excellence. That layer is built, tested, and delivered as a live dashboard covering five modules and five KPIs across 50 machines and 99,972 validated readings.

Applied to this corpus, it returns an unambiguous verdict. The nine sensor channels are mutually independent to within sampling error. The efficiency target is a two-variable arithmetic rule, recovered exactly and reproducing the published label 99.998% of the time. The physical sensors predict efficiency at precisely chance level and perform identically against randomly shuffled labels. Two structural defects — a duplicated capture day and a factory-wide rather than per-machine sampling cadence — compound the problem.

The corpus therefore cannot support predictive maintenance, efficiency forecasting, or machine benchmarking. Reporting that, with reproducible evidence, is more valuable than a model that would have reported 77.8% accuracy while having learned nothing at all — and the distance between those two outcomes, measurable only through deliberate validation, is the central practical finding of this work.

For Thales and its industrial partners, the path forward is concrete: fix the sampling cadence and timeline integrity, source efficiency labels independently of the feature table, verify that physical coupling survives the ingestion pipeline, and gate every future dataset through the six-check protocol in Section 7.3. The analytical layer is ready and will run unchanged against instrumented telemetry. What it needs is data that measures the factory.
